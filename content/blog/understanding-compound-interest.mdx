Albert Einstein famously described compound interest as the greatest mathematical discovery of all time, remarking: *"He who understands it, earns it; he who doesn't, pays it."* While simple interest generates returns strictly on the principal deposit, compound interest generates interest on both initial principal and the accumulated interest from prior periods.

## The Standard Compounding Equation

The compound interest formula calculates future accumulated balance:

$$A = P \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$$

Where:
- **A** = Final balance after compounding
- **P** = Initial principal balance
- **r** = Annual interest rate (in decimal format, e.g. 7% = 0.07)
- **n** = Compounding frequency per year (12 for monthly, 4 for quarterly, 1 for annually)
- **t** = Time investment is held in years

## The Famous "Rule of 72"

When planning long-term financial freedom, you can estimate how many years it will take to double your money using the **Rule of 72**:

$$\text{Years to Double} \approx \frac{72}{\text{Annual Interest Rate}}$$

For example, an investment growing at **8% per year** will double roughly every **9 years** (72 ÷ 8 = 9). At **12% per year**, your money doubles every **6 years**.

## The Power of Starting Early

Consider two investors, Maya and Liam:
- **Maya** invests $5,000 annually starting at age 22. She stops contributing at age 32 (10 years total = $50,000 invested).
- **Liam** starts investing $5,000 annually at age 32 and continues every single year until age 62 (30 years total = $150,000 invested).

Assuming both earn a steady 8% annualized return, by age 62, **Maya has over $800,000**, while Liam has approximately **$610,000**. Even though Maya invested only one-third of the capital Liam contributed, her additional decade of exponential compounding produced a much larger nest egg.

## Harnessing Compound Interest in Your Portfolio

1. **Reinvest Dividends and Interest**: Do not withdraw your periodic gains; let them snowball.
2. **Increase Contribution Frequencies**: Monthly systematic investment plans (SIPs) beat lump sums by dollar-cost averaging market volatility.
3. **Minimize Unnecessary Fees**: High management fees act like reverse compounding on your portfolio returns.
